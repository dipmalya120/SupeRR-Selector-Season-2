Six numbers worth remembering

How we define pressure

"Clutch" is usually a feeling dressed up as a stat. We made it a number for every ball in 200,999 deliveries (200,961 once 38 super-over balls are set aside). Start with the map of the whole method, then walk through each idea in order.

The method at a glance

TipClick any box to open that step, with its charts, assumptions and checks, on the Method page.

1Start with the scoreboard: how likely is a win?

A win-probability model reads score, wickets, balls left, target, team strength, venue and era, and states the batting side's chance before every ball. It is always scored on matches it never saw (calibration is on the Method page).

2Leverage: how much can the next ball move the match?

Leverage is the expected size of the next ball's win-chance swing, scaled so an average ball is 1.00. A ball at 3.0 can move the match three times as much as a typical one.

Where leverage lives in a chase
Each cell is the average leverage at that win chance (rows) and over (columns). Outlined cells clear our 1.23× pressure line. TipHover a cell for its ball count.
The critic's read
Pressure is not "late overs" and it is not "chasing". It lives where the contest is live (win chance 30% to 70%) and late (overs 17 to 20). A ball at 5% or 95% barely matters, however late it is.
That is why we score every ball on its own merits instead of tagging overs as "death" and calling it pressure.

4Pressure for a player: high leverage and his side is not cruising

A batter only feels pressure if his side is not already winning easily; a bowler only if the batting side is not already beaten. So a ball counts for the batter at 60% win chance or less, and for the bowler at 40% or more.

6Pressure is the moment; stakes are the fixture

We also measured stakes: how far a match moves a team's title chance (4,000 simulated season remainders for league games; fixed ladder weights 0.25 / 0.25 / 0.5 / 1.0 for the playoffs). Stakes are measured and reported, but never used to weight a rating.

The critic's read
So a rating that simply loaded knockout games would be measuring the fixture, not the player. We keep the two ideas apart.

7From a pressure ball to a rating

Each pressure ball is judged against par: what a typical player would do in that exact situation (phase, wickets, target, ground, pace or spin, hands, team strength, era; player identity left out). Value = runs beyond par plus a wicket price that depends on the phase.

value of a ball = (runs − par) ± wicket price × (wicket − expected wicket chance)
rating = average value × 100 balls (batters) or × 24 balls (bowlers), then shrunk toward the group

Stress tests: do the picks survive being poked?

A rating is only worth using if it holds up when you lean on it. Here are the pokes, and what each one showed.

1The 2-of-4 rule

Every rank is recomputed at four pressure cutoffs. To be picked, a player must be top 10 in at least two. Green = top 10 at that cutoff.

2Is "variety" the secret to the death overs?

A popular theory says bowlers who mix their deliveries save more runs at the death. We tested it on all 131 bowlers with enough death balls.

The critic's verdict
So variety is not used to pick bowlers. We keep it as description only: it shows how a bowler wins, not whether he does.

3Is pressure just everyday form?

Each dot is a player: raw value on pressure balls against raw value on ordinary balls, before shrinkage. Above the line = better when it matters. TipToggle batters and bowlers.

How to read it
Dots on the diagonal play the same either way. Distance from it is the resilience gap. We report it, and blends of level with resilience or consistency were run as checks, but the ranking uses the shrunk level only.

6What this can and cannot tell you

Wide CIsPressure balls are few per player, so we show an 80% CI and a chance of top 10, not a single number.
Soft below Tier AOrder inside Tiers B and C is soft: neighbouring ranks often sit within noise of each other.
Low split-half reliabilityOne half of a player's pressure balls only weakly predicts the other half (0.10 for batters, 0.02 for bowlers). That is why we shrink and use the 2-of-4 rule.
